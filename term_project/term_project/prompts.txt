When designing my own game "Singleton Files" (a detective logic deduction puzzle game), I initially asked AI to generate the structure for me and tried to go off that. Somewhere along the way, I realized how much refactoring I was doing and how many times I had to babysit and guide it. It really liked to shove all the logic into one massive class file and couldn't grasp encapsulation or separation of concerns even when prompted. It could be a problem with my prompting, but after a day I ended up scrapping that initial route and just doing my own structure, defining all my classes, interfaces, etc. After doing that, it was a lot easier and smooth sailing. I was able to direct it way easier, though I still had to check all of its work. Overall, the experience was great. I can definitely see the potential in having AI work alongside me as a developer in the future.

One of the best strategies I ended up using was jumping around between different AIs for different things. I used Google Gemini, Claude, and NotebookLM. Gemini was great at generating the initial design mockups and terminal layouts. Claude was super helpful with visualization and explaining complicated parts of the code so I could actually wrap my head around them. But NotebookLM was really great for enforcing the strict COMP 2522 style guides. Since I fed it the Java Coding Standards Cheat Sheet, it only grabbed knowledge from there. It caught and cleaned up all the bad practices the other AIs kept ignoring (even when I prompted). My workflow basically became a loop: I’d ask NotebookLM for a prompt, feed that to Gemini or Claude, take what they gave me, add my own tweaks, and toss it back into NotebookLM to make sure it didn't break any course rules. I just looped that until I was happy.

The best part of the whole experience was the AI's ability to do all the gruesome, repetitive tasks for me. Also, I made the AI do the styling and the style fixes because I absolutely hate CSS. Being able to step back and be genuinely happy (aesthetically and visually) with how my project turned out was definitely one of the highlights. I just think an app isn't fully complete until you visually see it all come together.

There came a point where I was constantly fighting the AI when it tried to break basic structured programming rules or throw in stuff we never even learned in class, even terrible practices to provide a “band-aid fix” to a part of a code. Like, when I was parsing the text files in my World and Score classes, the AI kept spitting out loops full of "continue" and "break" statements. I had to keep telling it to rewrite the logic. Sometimes it wouldn’t choose the “better” way to do something too. AI’s always preferred super long if-else-if.

It also sucked at the "No Magic Numbers" or creating symbolic constants for certain cases like an exit char, delimiter, etc. It was fine at extracting UI sizes into constants, but it kept leaving stuff like -1 in indexOf() checks or using ":" directly in the code. I had to constantly step in and force it to make constants like INDEX_NOT_FOUND. It also kept trying to use complex Math.round() algorithms to format my score decimals instead of just using a simple String.format().

One of the biggest lessons I learned is that you can't just tell an AI what to do as a broad idea. It needs specific steps and strict rules, exactly like how you would write code. That's really why prompt and context engineering is so powerful. If you just feed it a loose concept, it loses context pretty fast and just starts hallucinating random stuff. There definitely comes a point where you start getting more frustrated trying to fix its hallucinations versus having it actually help you.

Overall, working with AI saved me a lot of time on boilerplate code and styling, but you really have to supervise it. It’s a tool, not the solution. There are definitely better ways to utilize AI as well to get better results with an agentic setup but that’s way too much for the scope of this project. Using AI straight up how it is offered to the general public, you can never just accept it blindly. Always check over its work, and double check it again. AI is a crazy powerful tool for writing code, when given the scope and proper rules.
